# Task 3 — risk notes (synthetic)

## What the task is

Given a contract clause and its category, produce **one sentence** telling a
business reader what the clause does in practice and what brings it into
effect. Input is the clause, not the whole contract, which makes the three
tasks chain: Task 2 locates the clause, Task 3 explains it. Feeding a whole
contract to write a note about one clause would also inherit Task 2's
truncation problem for no benefit.

## Provenance, stated plainly

This is the only **synthetic** task of the three. There is no human-annotated
source. Notes are generated by **Qwen3-32B** (Apache-2.0), run on our own
hardware — no frontier API, no third-party terms attached to the output. One
note per CUAD row where the clause is present, splits inherited structurally
from the source contract so no contract appears on both sides of a split.

Seed 20260919, recorded in the manifest. Decoding is sampled but each request
carries its own seed derived from that value, so the run is reproducible.

A model trained on this learns to imitate Qwen3-32B's explanations. It does not
learn law. Any claim about the quality of these notes is a claim about the
teacher, and the ceiling is the teacher's ceiling.

## The first attempt was degenerate, and the filters said it was perfect

The first 40-row smoke reported **40/40 kept, 100% pass rate**. Measuring the
output instead of trusting that number:

| measure | first attempt |
|---|---|
| notes opening with the identical 6 words | 34 / 40 (**85.0%**) |
| distinct 4-word openings | 5 / 40 |
| distinct 6-word openings | 6 / 40 |
| sentences per note | 1 for all 40 |

Every note was a rewording of *"This clause matters to the business because…"*.

**Cause, in two parts.** The system prompt contained the literal phrase
"explains why this clause matters to the business", so the teacher copied the
instruction into its answer. And decoding was `temperature=0.0`, so one fixed
prompt plus argmax produced the same opening on every row by construction.

**Why no filter caught it.** The three filters were blind to templating. f1
counts sentences; f2 checks length; f3 checks that quoted spans appear in the
clause. A note can pass all three while being the two-hundredth copy of one
stem. f2 is worse than blind — its bounds are the p1/p99 of the very batch it
filters, so it keeps ~98% by definition and its contribution to the pass rate
is close to tautological. The 100% figure measured the wrong thing.

## The fix, and the measurement that shows it worked

Three changes: the prompt no longer supplies a stem and explicitly bans the
observed opening; decoding moved to `temperature=0.7, top_p=0.9` with a
per-request seed; and a fourth filter, f4, caps the share of kept notes sharing
any 5-word opening at 5% of the run.

| measure | before | after |
|---|---|---|
| kept | 40 / 40 (100.0%) | 116 / 120 (96.7%) |
| distinct 5-word openings | 6 / 40 (15%) | **104 / 116 (90%)** |
| most common opening share | **85.0%** | **2.6%** |
| f4 rejections | n/a | 0 (cap was 6, max seen 3) |

f4 never fired, which is the right outcome — the generation fix solved the
problem at source and the filter is a guard against regression, not a crutch
propping up bad output. The rejections that *did* occur are real defects: three
notes ran to two sentences, one came in under the length floor.

Note the direction of the pass rate. It went **down**, from 100% to 96.7%, and
that is the improvement. A filter that rejects nothing is not evidence of clean
data; it is usually evidence that it is measuring the wrong property.

## Filters, in order

| filter | rejects | rationale |
|---|---|---|
| f1 sentence count | not exactly one sentence | the task is defined as one sentence |
| f2 length | outside observed p1..p99, floor 8 words | trims outliers; bounds are data-derived, not hand-picked |
| f3 groundedness | a quoted span (≥8 chars) absent from the clause | a note must not quote text the clause does not contain |
| f4 templating | opening shared by >5% of kept notes | prevents learning a stem instead of reasoning |

f3 uses the same normalizer as every other comparison in this project
(`v1_contractnli/text_norm.py`), so groundedness here means the same thing it
means in the evidence metrics.

## Known limitations

- **The teacher is the ceiling.** No human verified these notes. A sample of 30
  is written to `RISK_NOTE_SAMPLES.md` for review, with the clause shown beside
  each note so accuracy can actually be judged.
- **f2 is near-tautological** on its own batch. It is retained as an outlier
  trimmer, which is all it honestly is.
- **f4 is order-dependent.** The first notes sharing an opening are kept and
  later ones rejected. Deterministic given fixed row order, but arbitrary as to
  which specific note survives.
- **No factual verification.** f3 checks that *quoted* text exists in the
  clause. It cannot check whether an unquoted paraphrase is accurate. A note
  that confidently misdescribes a clause in its own words passes every filter.
  That is the gap the human sample exists to probe.
